If you’ve just been told

If your provider has just told you that the pregnancy isn’t viable, the most important things to know:

  • This is almost never your fault. About half of early losses come from random chromosomal abnormalities of the embryo that have nothing to do with what you did or didn’t do.
  • You have time and options. Unless you’re hemodynamically unstable or have signs of infection, there is no medical urgency to choose treatment in the next hours. You can take days to decide.
  • Your preferences matter. Modern guidance is explicit that the choice between waiting and monitoring, taking medication, and having a surgical procedure should accommodate your preferences when no urgent indication exists.
  • Future fertility is usually unaffected after a single first-trimester loss. The chance of a healthy next pregnancy is roughly the same as your chance for any pregnancy.
  • Grief is normal and real. Pregnancy loss is associated with clinically significant rates of acute grief, depression, and post-traumatic symptoms[7]Herbert et al., The mental health impact of perinatal loss: A systematic review and..., J Affect Disord 2022 · PMID 34678403. Mental-health support is part of care, not a sign of weakness.

How an early loss is diagnosed

The diagnosis of early pregnancy loss usually combines transvaginal ultrasound findings and serum beta-hCG trends. Specific ultrasound criteria for non-viable pregnancy:

  • Crown-rump length (CRL) ≥ 7 mm without cardiac activity.
  • Mean sac diameter (MSD) ≥ 25 mm without an embryo.
  • Absence of embryo with heartbeat ≥ 2 weeks after a scan that showed a gestational sac without a yolk sac.
  • Absence of embryo with heartbeat ≥ 11 days after a scan that showed a gestational sac with a yolk sac.

Beta-hCG patterns help when ultrasound is inconclusive — rising hCG <35–50% over 48 hours is concerning for a non-viable pregnancy or ectopic. One single set of numbers is rarely enough — serial measurements and a follow-up ultrasound are the standard before declaring a pregnancy non-viable.

What to ask for if you’re unsure of the diagnosis:

  • A copy of the ultrasound report.
  • A second opinion (formal repeat scan, sometimes a different operator).
  • Time — if the diagnosis is borderline, repeating the scan in 7–14 days often clarifies.

The three management options

When the diagnosis is clear and there’s no urgent issue (heavy bleeding, infection, hemodynamic concern), three options are available. None is medically “right” or “wrong”; the choice depends on your preferences, your timeline, and your medical history.

1

Wait and monitor

Let the loss complete on its own, over days to weeks. Limited to the first trimester. Most natural-feeling option for many.

2

Treatment with medication

Mifepristone followed by misoprostol — achieves complete expulsion in ~84% of patients[2]Schreiber et al., Mifepristone Pretreatment for the Medical Management of Early Pregn..., N Engl J Med 2018 · PMID 29874535, much higher than misoprostol alone. At home.

3

Surgical treatment

Office or OR D&C / suction evacuation. Fastest resolution. Highest success rate. Local or moderate sedation depending on setting.

E

Ectopic pregnancy

A separate pathway — pregnancy implanted outside the uterus. Most common patient questions

Did I do something wrong?

Almost certainly not. About half of first-trimester losses are caused by random chromosomal abnormalities of the embryo that occurred at conception and have nothing to do with what you did, ate, lifted, drank, or experienced emotionally. Working out, sex, mild illnesses, mild stress, common medications — none of these cause early pregnancy loss.

What does my age have to do with it?

Maternal age strongly affects miscarriage risk because egg quality declines with age. Loss rates rise from 9–17% at age 20–30 to 40% at age 40 and 80% at age 45. This is biology, not anything you can change — but it does affect the conversation about future pregnancy and any evaluation you might want.

How will I know I’ve passed the pregnancy?

Bleeding becomes heavier than menstrual flow, cramping is significant, and you may pass tissue (often described as “like beef liver” or a clot-with-grayish-tissue). Pain usually peaks during passage and improves substantially within hours. Bleeding tapers to lighter flow over 1–2 weeks. A follow-up ultrasound at 1–2 weeks usually confirms completion.

When can I try again?

Physically, most couples can try again with the next menstrual cycle — usually 4–6 weeks after the loss. There is no medical reason to wait longer for one early loss. Emotionally, the right time varies enormously by person and couple — some are ready right away, others need months. Both are normal.

Do I need RhoGAM (RhIg)?

Per the 2024 ACOG clinical practice update[6]?, ACOG Clinical Practice Update: Rh D Immune Globulin Administration ..., Obstet Gynecol 2024 · PMID 39255498: routine RhIg is no longer needed for pregnancy loss or abortion before 12 0/7 weeks — flow cytometry data show 99.8% of patients remain below the sensitization threshold. For loss at or beyond 12 weeks, RhIg is still indicated in Rh-negative women. Some patients with Rh-negative blood and plans for future pregnancy still choose to receive RhIg even at <12 weeks via shared decision-making.

How many losses before we do a workup?

Two or more clinical pregnancy losses meets the modern definition of recurrent pregnancy loss[3]Practice Committee of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine. The workup includes evaluation for antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, an assessment of the uterine cavity (sonohysterography or hysteroscopy), karyotype of pregnancy tissue when available, parental karyotypes in selected cases, and an endocrine workup (thyroid, prolactin, diabetes, sometimes prolactin and progesterone).

Will this affect future pregnancies?

After a single first-trimester loss, the chance of a healthy next pregnancy is roughly the same as your chance was for the lost pregnancy — the lost pregnancy doesn’t add risk to the next one. After recurrent losses (2+), the chance of a healthy next pregnancy is still good, but lower — about 60–70% even after 3–4 losses. Workup can sometimes identify treatable causes.

How do I cope emotionally?

Grief after pregnancy loss is real and clinically significant[7]Herbert et al., The mental health impact of perinatal loss: A systematic review and..., J Affect Disord 2022 · PMID 34678403 — reactions range from quiet sadness to clinical depression to features of acute stress disorder. None of these are signs of being “weak” or “broken.” Options that help: peer support (groups, online communities), counseling with a perinatal-loss-aware therapist, ritual or memorialization when meaningful to you, time. If you’re struggling more than a few weeks — or if you have thoughts of self-harm — please reach out. The portal messaging is one channel; crisis lines and your primary care team are others.

    MAIN RESULTS: We included two trials involving over 4500 women, comparing anti-D prophylaxis with no anti-D during pregnancy in this review. Overall, the trials were judged to be at moderate to high risk of bias. The quality of the evidence for pre-specified outcomes was also assessed using the GRADE (Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach.In regards to primary review outcomes, there did not appear to be a clear difference in the risks of immunisation when women who received anti-D at 28 and 34 weeks' gestation were compared with women who were not given antenatal anti-D: risk ratio (RR) for incidence of Rhesus D alloimmunisation during pregnancy was 0.42 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.15 to 1.17, two trials, 3902 women; GRADE: low quality evidence); at birth of a Rh-positive infant the RR was 0.42 (95% CI 0.15 to 1.17, two trials, 2297 women); and within 12 months after birth of a Rh-positive infant the average RR was 0.39 (95% CI 0.10 to 1.62, two trials, 2048 women; Tau²: 0.47; I²: 39%; GRADE: low quality evidence). Neither of the trials reported on incidence of Rhesus D alloimmunisation in subsequent pregnancies.Considering secondary outcomes, in one trial, women receiving anti-D during pregnancy were shown to be less likely to register a positive Kleihauer test (which detects fetal cells in maternal blood) in pregnancy (at 32 to 25 weeks) (RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.88; 1884 women; GRADE: low quality evidence) and at the birth of a Rh-positive infant (RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.46 to 0.79; 1189 women; GRADE: low quality evidence). No clear differences were seen for neonatal jaundice (RR 0.26, 95% CI 0.03 to 2.30; 1882 infants; GRADE: very low quality evidence). Neither of the trials reported on adverse effects associated with anti-D treatment.

    AUTHORS' CONCLUSIONS: Existing studies do not provide conclusive evidence that the use of anti-D during pregnancy benefits either mother or baby in terms of incidence of Rhesus D alloimmunisation during the pregnancy or postpartum, or the incidence of neonatal morbidity (jaundice) (low to very low quality evidence). However women receiving anti-D may be less likely to register a positive Kleihauer test in pregnancy and at the birth of a Rh-positive infant (low quality evidence). Fewer women who receive anti-D during pregnancy may have Rhesus D antibodies in a subsequent pregnancy, with benefits for the baby, however this needs to be tested in studies of robust design.
