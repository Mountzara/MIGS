What it actually is

Tissue that belongs in one place, growing in another — and behaving badly.

Every month, the lining of your uterus (the endometrium) thickens and then sheds during your period. In endometriosis, tissue that looks and behaves like that lining ends up outside the uterus — on the ovaries, on the ligaments that hold the uterus in place, on the bladder, the bowel, the lining of the pelvis. Each month, that tissue still tries to bleed[1]?, Diagnosis of Endometriosis, Obstet Gynecol 2026 · PMID 41712950. But it has nowhere to go.

That trapped bleeding causes inflammation, scarring (called adhesions), and over time, deeply sensitized pain pathways. Endometriosis is not a disease of unusually heavy or painful periods alone — it is an estrogen-driven, inflammatory condition that grows and changes over years if it is not treated[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458.

Why it hurts

Four overlapping pain sources, all happening at the same time.

One of the reasons endometriosis is so often under-treated is that pain in this condition is never coming from just one place. Treating only the lesions — with hormones or even with surgery — leaves the other three layers untreated, which is why so many women continue to hurt after “the surgery went well.” Click any card to read the deeper dive on that layer.

The treatment ladder

Most women never need surgery. Here is how we get there.

Endometriosis is a chronic, estrogen-dependent condition. Most treatments work by reducing or eliminating monthly cycles, which both calms current symptoms and slows disease progression[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458[6]Zakhari et al., Endometriosis recurrence following post-operative hormonal suppress..., Hum Reprod Update 2021 · PMID 33020832. We move up the ladder one step at a time, judging each on response over 3–6 months. Click any rung to read what that step involves in detail — including risks, benefits, and alternatives.

01

NSAIDs + continuous hormonal contraception

First-line for almost everyone. NSAIDs taken pre-emptively 1–2 days before period (more effective than waiting until pain starts). Continuous combined OCP suppresses the cycle entirely[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458.

naproxen 500 mg BID · continuous OCP (norethindrone/EE 1 mg / 20 mcg)

The osteopathic layer

OMT does not replace anything you are already doing. It addresses what they can’t.

This is a DO-led MIGS practice — a fully licensed gynecologic surgeon with additional training in osteopathic manipulative treatment. For pelvic pain, OMT addresses the structural and neurological contributors that hormones and surgery don’t directly reach, layered on top of standard care rather than instead of it.

From the clinic counseling script, verbatim:

In endometriosis, pain comes from at least four overlapping sources simultaneously: the lesions themselves releasing inflammatory chemicals like prostaglandins and cytokines; the nerves around the lesions, which become abnormally sensitive over time; the pelvic-floor muscles, which develop trigger points and tightness as a guarding response; and the fascia and ligaments — especially the broad ligament and uterosacral ligaments — which can become restricted by adhesions and inflammation. Hormonal therapy reduces the activity of the lesions but does not directly address the muscle tightness, the fascial restriction, or the sensitized pain pathways — which is why some women continue to have pain even after good hormonal control or after excision surgery.

The 8‑week OMT protocol for endometriosis targets each layer separately: high-velocity-low-amplitude techniques at the T12–L2 spinal levels (where the nerves to the uterus and ovaries enter the spinal cord), bilateral sacroiliac treatment to normalize parasympathetic supply to the pelvis, direct broad-ligament mobilization to release fascial restriction, and pelvic-diaphragm release to reduce pelvic-floor tension[8]Muñoz-Gómez et al., Effectiveness of a Manual Therapy Protocol in Women with Pelvic Pai..., J Clin Med 2023 · PMID 37176750. The full hormonal regimen, NSAIDs, pelvic-floor PT, and any planned surgery continue unchanged.

Common questions, answered honestly

Patient questions, in the order they come up.

Sources for the answers below: ACOG and ESHRE clinical guidelines, the Cochrane and network-meta-analysis literature on surgical and medical management, and the randomized-trial evidence base for osteopathic and pelvic-floor treatment. Every claim has an inline citation.

Why has my diagnosis taken so long?

You are not imagining it: the average time between first symptoms and diagnosis is 7–10 years[3]Vercellini et al., Association of endometriosis and adenomyosis with pregnancy and inf..., Fertil Steril 2023 · PMID 36948440. This happens because endometriosis pain is often dismissed as “normal” period pain; because symptoms overlap with IBS, bladder problems, and musculoskeletal pain; because for decades laparoscopic surgery was required to confirm the diagnosis; and because ultrasound, when not specifically focused on endometriosis, misses superficial disease entirely[5]Guerriero et al., Transvaginal ultrasound vs magnetic resonance imaging for diagnosin..., Ultrasound Obstet Gynecol 2018 · PMID 29154402.

The good news: ACOG’s most recent guidance explicitly says a clinical diagnosis is appropriate[1]?, Diagnosis of Endometriosis, Obstet Gynecol 2026 · PMID 41712950 — meaning we no longer need a confirmatory surgery before starting treatment.

Do I need surgery to know if I have endometriosis?

No. ACOG[1]?, Diagnosis of Endometriosis, Obstet Gynecol 2026 · PMID 41712950 and ESHRE[14]Becker et al., ESHRE guideline: endometriosis, Hum Reprod Open 2022 · PMID 35350465 both now recommend that diagnosis can be made clinically — based on the symptom pattern, examination, and imaging — without requiring laparoscopy first. Diagnostic surgery still has a role for women who fail medical therapy, or when surgery would be the treatment anyway. But it is not the entry point.

Will hormonal therapy cure my endometriosis?

No. Hormonal therapy is suppressive — it controls symptoms and slows progression but does not eliminate the disease. The published evidence shows medical suppressive therapy improves pain in most women, but recurrence rates are high after the medication is stopped[6]Zakhari et al., Endometriosis recurrence following post-operative hormonal suppress..., Hum Reprod Update 2021 · PMID 33020832. The honest framing is: we are managing a chronic condition, not curing it.

For most women, that management continues until menopause or until they want to try to conceive. Step therapy gives us multiple options to rotate through, so that a side effect on one medication doesn’t end the conversation.

If hormones don’t cure it, why bother?

Three reasons. First, 60–80% of women have meaningful pain relief on first-line therapy[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458 — without surgery, recovery time, or surgical complications. Second, hormonal suppression slows disease progression: untreated endometriosis worsens in roughly 29% of cases, including new endometrioma formation, deeper disease, and adhesions that compromise fertility[3]Vercellini et al., Association of endometriosis and adenomyosis with pregnancy and inf..., Fertil Steril 2023 · PMID 36948440. Third, getting symptom control now reduces the central pain sensitization that makes future treatment harder[7]?, Chronic Pelvic Pain: ACOG Practice Bulletin Summary, Number 218, Obstet Gynecol 2020 · PMID 32080045.

Will it affect my fertility?

Endometriosis affects fertility in roughly 30–50% of women who have it, and it is found in about 38% of women evaluated for infertility[3]Vercellini et al., Association of endometriosis and adenomyosis with pregnancy and inf..., Fertil Steril 2023 · PMID 36948440[4]Hodgson et al., Interventions for endometriosis-related infertility: a systematic r..., Fertil Steril 2020 · PMID 32106991. Most women with endometriosis still conceive without help — especially with early diagnosis — but for those who don’t, the issue is usually a combination of pelvic adhesions, ovarian-reserve impact from endometriomas, and the inflammatory pelvic environment.

If you have endometriosis and you are thinking about pregnancy: raise it at your visit early. The network meta-analysis evidence supports that surgical management improves clinical pregnancy rates in endometriosis-related infertility[4]Hodgson et al., Interventions for endometriosis-related infertility: a systematic r..., Fertil Steril 2020 · PMID 32106991. We check AMH up front when there’s an endometrioma, so we know what we’re working with.

What exactly is OMT, and how is it different from chiropractic care or massage?

OMT — osteopathic manipulative treatment — is performed by licensed physicians (DOs and MDs with additional training). It uses a range of hands-on techniques (spinal manipulation, visceral mobilization, fascial release, counterstrain, muscle energy) integrated into full medical care: the same physician can perform OMT, prescribe your medications, and operate on you when needed.

Chiropractic care focuses on spinal manipulation; chiropractors are not licensed to prescribe medications or perform medical procedures. Massage targets broad muscle relaxation but does not treat the spinal segmental, fascial, or visceral contributors that drive pelvic pain.

OMT specifically has published randomized-trial evidence in pelvic pain and dysmenorrhea[8]Muñoz-Gómez et al., Effectiveness of a Manual Therapy Protocol in Women with Pelvic Pai..., J Clin Med 2023 · PMID 37176750[9]Molins-Cubero et al., Changes in pain perception after pelvis manipulation in women with ..., Pain Med 2014 · PMID 24666560[10]Ruffini et al., Osteopathic manipulative treatment in gynecology and obstetrics: A ..., Complement Ther Med 2016 · PMID 27261985[11]Alboni et al., Effects of osteopathic manipulative therapy on recurrent pelvic pai..., Minerva Obstet Gynecol 2024 · PMID 37997320 — click the trial cards above to read the actual studies.

Will the OMT hurt?

Most techniques are gentle and should not be painful. High-velocity-low-amplitude (HVLA) thrust techniques produce a brief sensation — sometimes with an audible pop — but should not be sharp or painful. If a technique is causing pain, it is modified immediately. The most common side effect is mild soreness for 24–48 hours afterward, similar to post-exercise soreness. Published pelvic-pain OMT trials report no serious adverse events[8]Muñoz-Gómez et al., Effectiveness of a Manual Therapy Protocol in Women with Pelvic Pai..., J Clin Med 2023 · PMID 37176750[9]Molins-Cubero et al., Changes in pain perception after pelvis manipulation in women with ..., Pain Med 2014 · PMID 24666560[10]Ruffini et al., Osteopathic manipulative treatment in gynecology and obstetrics: A ..., Complement Ther Med 2016 · PMID 27261985.

Why OMT if I’m already in pelvic-floor physical therapy?

Pelvic-floor PT and OMT address different structural layers. PFPT directly works on the pelvic-floor muscles themselves — through internal manual therapy, biofeedback, and down-training of the levator ani. OMT addresses the upstream contributors: the lower lumbar spine where the nerves to the uterus connect, the sacroiliac joints, the iliopsoas muscle that pre-tensions the pelvic floor at rest, and the broad-ligament adhesions that pull on the pelvis mechanically[8]Muñoz-Gómez et al., Effectiveness of a Manual Therapy Protocol in Women with Pelvic Pai..., J Clin Med 2023 · PMID 37176750.

The two are complementary. The NIH multicenter trial showed pelvic-floor PT alone helped 59% of women with pelvic-floor tenderness, compared with 26% on general massage[12]FitzGerald et al., Randomized multicenter clinical trial of myofascial physical therap..., J Urol 2012 · PMID 22503015. OMT adds the structural layer above that.

How long until I see improvement?

Hormonal therapy: 4–6 weeks for early signal; 3 months for a fair trial. If first-line is not working at 3 months, we escalate[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458.

OMT: mild post-session soreness in the first 2–3 sessions is normal. Meaningful pain improvement typically emerges at sessions 4–6 (weeks 4–6). Full protocol effect is assessed at week 8 — and in the published endometriosis trial, improvements were maintained at one-month follow-up[8]Muñoz-Gómez et al., Effectiveness of a Manual Therapy Protocol in Women with Pelvic Pai..., J Clin Med 2023 · PMID 37176750.

Pelvic-floor PT: usually 8–12 sessions before the muscle pattern shifts meaningfully[12]FitzGerald et al., Randomized multicenter clinical trial of myofascial physical therap..., J Urol 2012 · PMID 22503015.

Surgery: immediate pain reduction for some, longer recovery for others. The post-op 3-month mark is the real read[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458.

What happens if medical therapy doesn’t work for me?

We escalate. The standard step-up: first-line OCP or NSAIDs → second-line progestin-only or LNG-IUD → third-line GnRH antagonist with add-back hormones. If you have given two adequate trials (3–6 months each) and still have functional pain, that is when surgical evaluation moves to the front of the conversation.

Surgery for endometriosis is laparoscopic excision — cutting out the lesions, freeing adhesions, treating endometriomas by cystectomy (not just draining them). Excision rather than ablation[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458. Pain relief afterwards is about 60–80%. Recurrence is 20–40% over five years and drops to 8–15% with continued hormonal suppression post-op[6]Zakhari et al., Endometriosis recurrence following post-operative hormonal suppress..., Hum Reprod Update 2021 · PMID 33020832 — which is why we usually place an LNG-IUD at the time of surgery.

What’s the difference between excision and ablation?

Ablation burns the surface of the lesion away. Excision cuts the lesion out entirely, including the deeper tissue you can’t see from the surface. For ovarian endometriomas the published evidence is clear: excising the cyst wall (cystectomy) is superior to draining and ablating it — lower recurrence and better symptom relief[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458. For peritoneal disease, excision is also preferred when technically feasible. Excision is performed laparoscopically as the default approach.

Will it come back after surgery?

Some of it can. Recurrence is roughly 20–40% at five years without ongoing therapy — and drops to 8–15% with post-operative hormonal suppression[6]Zakhari et al., Endometriosis recurrence following post-operative hormonal suppress..., Hum Reprod Update 2021 · PMID 33020832. That suppression is usually an LNG-IUD (placed at the time of surgery) or a continuous combined OCP, continued until menopause or until you want to try to conceive. Skipping post-op suppression is the most common reason for symptomatic recurrence in the published series.

Is surgery safe?

Laparoscopic excision is generally low-risk when performed by a surgeon with subspecialty training in MIGS. Specific risks include bleeding, infection, injury to nearby organs (bowel, bladder, ureter, blood vessels), formation of new adhesions, and — in surgery on the ovary itself — reduction in ovarian reserve[2]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458. These risks are taken seriously and minimized by careful technique, intraoperative checks (cystoscopy when the bladder or ureter has been close to the surgical field), and by avoiding aggressive ovarian dissection when fertility is a priority.

Is OMT covered by insurance?

Yes, in most cases. OMT is billed under CPT codes 98925–98929 and is covered by most insurance plans including Medicare and Medicaid when paired with the appropriate somatic-dysfunction diagnosis codes. Same-day office visit and OMT can be billed together. Deductible and co-insurance apply per your specific plan — our office will check your coverage in advance and tell you what to expect.

I think the pain is in my head. Could that be it?

It is not “in your head,” but the nervous system is part of the story — in a real, physical way. Long-standing pain rewires the spinal cord and brain to amplify pain signals (central sensitization)[7]?, Chronic Pelvic Pain: ACOG Practice Bulletin Summary, Number 218, Obstet Gynecol 2020 · PMID 32080045. That is why some women continue to have pain even after the visible lesions are gone, and why a multi-modal approach — hormones plus pelvic-floor PT plus OMT plus, sometimes, neuromodulators like low-dose amitriptyline or gabapentin — is more effective than any single therapy alone. Acknowledging the nervous-system component is not dismissing your pain. It is the opposite: it is taking it seriously enough to actually address it.

When to call urgently

Symptoms that should not wait for your next visit.

Call the office or seek care now if you have:
  • Sudden, severe one-sided pelvic pain — especially with nausea or vomiting (possible ovarian torsion)
  • Pain that prescribed medications are no longer touching
  • Bleeding heavy enough to soak through a pad or tampon every hour for two consecutive hours
  • Fever above 101°F with pelvic pain
  • New bowel symptoms — rectal bleeding, severe constipation, or pain with bowel movements that wasn’t there before
  • New urinary symptoms — blood in the urine, inability to fully empty the bladder
  • Pain so severe you cannot work, sleep, or function
  • If you suspect you are pregnant and you are on hormonal therapy
