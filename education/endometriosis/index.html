What it actually is

Tissue that belongs in one place, growing in another — and behaving badly.

Every month, the lining of your uterus (the endometrium) thickens and then sheds during your period. In endometriosis, tissue that looks and behaves like that lining ends up outside the uterus — on the ovaries, on the ligaments that hold the uterus in place, on the bladder, the bowel, the lining of the pelvis. Why it hurts

Four overlapping pain sources, all happening at the same time.

One of the reasons endometriosis is so often under-treated is that pain in this condition is never coming from just one place. Treating only the lesions — with hormones or even with surgery — leaves the other three layers untreated, which is why so many women continue to hurt after “the surgery went well.” Click any card to read the deeper dive on that layer.

The osteopathic layer

OMT does not replace anything you are already doing. It addresses what they can’t.

This is a DO-led CBG/MIGS practice — a fully licensed gynecologic surgeon with additional training in osteopathic manipulative treatment. For pelvic pain, OMT addresses the structural and neurological contributors that hormones and surgery don’t directly reach, layered on top of standard care rather than instead of it.

From the clinic counseling script, verbatim:

In endometriosis, pain comes from at least four overlapping sources simultaneously: the lesions themselves releasing inflammatory chemicals like prostaglandins and cytokines; the nerves around the lesions, which become abnormally sensitive over time; the pelvic-floor muscles, which develop trigger points and tightness as a guarding response; and the fascia and ligaments — especially the broad ligament and uterosacral ligaments — which can become restricted by adhesions and inflammation. What exactly is OMT, and how is it different from chiropractic care or massage?

OMT — osteopathic manipulative treatment — is performed by licensed physicians (DOs and MDs with additional training). It uses a range of hands-on techniques (spinal manipulation, visceral mobilization, fascial release, counterstrain, muscle energy) integrated into full medical care: the same physician can perform OMT, prescribe your medications, and operate on you when needed.

Chiropractic care focuses on spinal manipulation; chiropractors are not licensed to prescribe medications or perform medical procedures. Massage targets broad muscle relaxation but does not treat the spinal segmental, fascial, or visceral contributors that drive pelvic pain.

When to call urgently

Symptoms that should not wait for your next visit.

Call the office or seek care now if you have:
  • Sudden, severe one-sided pelvic pain — especially with nausea or vomiting (possible ovarian torsion)
  • Pain that prescribed medications are no longer touching
  • Bleeding heavy enough to soak through a pad or tampon every hour for two consecutive hours
  • Fever above 101°F with pelvic pain
  • New bowel symptoms — rectal bleeding, severe constipation, or pain with bowel movements that wasn’t there before
  • New urinary symptoms — blood in the urine, inability to fully empty the bladder
  • Pain so severe you cannot work, sleep, or function
  • If you suspect you are pregnant and you are on hormonal therapy

  14. Eberle et al., Medical Management of Ovarian Endometriomas: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis, Obstet Gynecol 2024
    Hormone therapy genuinely shrinks ovarian endometriomas, so surgery is not the only way to reduce cyst size. Pooled across 33 studies, dienogest cut mean cyst diameter by about 1.3 cm, the oral contraceptive pill by about 1.1 cm, gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists by about 1.2 cm, norethindrone acetate by 0.6 cm and danazol by about 2 cm. Each drug rests on only two to nine small studies, and trials of surgery, or of recurrence after surgery, were excluded by design.
    PMID 37944155
    Read the abstract
    OBJECTIVE: To estimate the effect of medical management on the size of ovarian endometriomas.

    DATA SOURCE: Online databases were searched from inception to October 2022, including Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid EMBASE, PubMed, EBM Reviews-Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), ClinicalTrials.gov , and Web of Science.

    METHODS OF STUDY SELECTION: Following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines, we included all English-language, full-text articles that reported on change in endometrioma size (either diameter or volume) after medical interventions. Studies evaluating surgical interventions or postoperative recurrence were excluded. All screening and data extraction were performed independently by two authors. Risk of bias assessment was performed with either the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomized controlled trials or a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies.

    TABULATION, INTEGRATION, AND RESULTS: After removal of duplicates, 9,332 studies were screened, with 33 full-text articles deemed eligible for inclusion. In the meta-analysis, dienogest showed significant reduction in cyst diameter (reduction 1.32 cm, 95% CI, 0.91-1.73, eight studies, n=418 cysts) and volume (mean difference of log-transformed volume 1.35, 95% CI, 0.87-1.83, seven studies, n=282 cysts). Similarly, significant reductions were seen with the oral contraceptive pill (OCP) (1.06 cm, 95% CI, 0.59-1.53, nine studies, n=455), gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists (1.17 cm, 95% CI, 0.42-1.92, four studies, n=128 cysts), norethindrone acetate (0.6 cm, 95% CI, 0.27-0.94, two studies, n=88 cysts), and danazol (1.95 cm, 95% CI, 1.18-2.73, two studies, n=34 cysts). Norethindrone acetate with aromatase inhibitor was also effective in reducing endometrioma volume (mean difference of log-transformed volume 1.47, 95% CI, 0.16-2.78, two studies, n=34 cysts).

    CONCLUSION: Medical management with dienogest, OCPs, GnRH agonists, norethindrone acetate, norethindrone acetate with aromatase inhibitor, or danazol can reduce the size of ovarian endometriomas.
