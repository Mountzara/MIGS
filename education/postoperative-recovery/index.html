The recovery timeline

Recovery is layered. The week before sets up how the day goes; the day of recovery sets up the first week; the first week sets up the longer return-to-everything. Each phase has its own focus, and there’s a dedicated card below for each.

The four things that drive most post-op calls

Most post-CBG/MIGS telephone calls are about constipation, pain, urinary catheters, and vaginal bleeding. Knowing what’s normal for each takes a lot of stress out of recovery.

Constipation

  • Expected for 3–7 days after surgery from anesthesia, decreased activity, and any opioid use.
  • Pre-empt it: start polyethylene glycol (Miralax) the day after surgery, plus stool softener (docusate). Adequate hydration and walking are non-negotiable.
  • If no bowel movement by day 4: add a stimulant laxative (senna, bisacodyl).
  • Call if no bowel movement by day 7 despite a full regimen, severe distension, or vomiting.

Pain

  • Predictable for the first 3–7 days. Worst at day 1–2, improving thereafter.
  • Foundation: scheduled acetaminophen 1000 mg every 8 hours + NSAID (ibuprofen 600 mg every 6–8 hours) with food[6]Massoth et al., Impact of opioid-free anaesthesia on postoperative nausea, vomiting..., J Clin Anesth 2021 · PMID 34229292.
  • Opioid (oxycodone or similar) only for breakthrough pain not relieved by the above.
  • Call if pain is escalating despite scheduled regimen, severely sharp / localized, or new and different.

Urinary issues

  • Postoperative urinary retention (POUR) can happen after pelvic surgery — impaired emptying despite a full bladder.
  • If you had a catheter overnight: removed before discharge in most cases; some patients go home with one for 24–48 hours.
  • Burning with urination (especially with concentrated urine from inadequate fluid intake) is common but warrants a urine culture if it persists.
  • Call if you can’t empty your bladder, have severe burning with cloudy or bloody urine, or develop fever.

Vaginal bleeding

  • Light spotting to light bleeding for 2–6 weeks after most gynecologic surgery is expected, depending on the procedure (hysterectomy, hysteroscopic surgery, vaginal procedures bleed more than purely laparoscopic).
  • The pattern: heaviest in the first days, gradually transitioning to brown / dark discharge, then minimal by week 2–3.
  • Use pads (not tampons) until cleared at your post-op visit.
  • Call if you soak through a pad every 1–2 hours, pass large clots, or have new heavy bleeding after it had stopped.

Most common patient questions

When can I drive again?

When you are off opioid pain medication, can comfortably perform an emergency stop, and feel mentally sharp. For most laparoscopic gynecologic procedures, that’s typically 5–7 days. After abdominal hysterectomy or larger open procedures, it can be 2–3 weeks. Use your judgment — if a sudden brake-slam would be a problem, you’re not ready.

When can I exercise / lift?

Light walking starts the day of surgery — it’s part of ERAS. Most patients return to light non-strenuous activity (yoga, easy cardio, light weights) by week 2. Heavy lifting (more than ~10–15 lb) is restricted for 4–6 weeks after laparoscopic surgery, longer after open abdominal procedures or extensive reconstruction. Full-intensity workouts at 6 weeks for most patients. Pelvic-floor and core work is often part of post-op recovery if you had vaginal or pelvic floor procedures.

When can I have sex?

After most laparoscopic gynecologic surgery — 2–4 weeks when there’s no vaginal cuff or vaginal repair to heal. After hysterectomy — nothing in the vagina (intercourse, tampons, douching) for 6 weeks to allow the vaginal cuff to heal. After vaginal reconstructive surgery — per the specific recommendation given at the post-op visit.

How do I know if I have an infection?

Surgical site infection is the most common gyn-surgery complication. Signs: fever >38.0°C (100.4°F), spreading redness around an incision, increasing pain after day 2–3, purulent (yellow / green / foul-smelling) drainage from an incision or vagina, severe abdominal pain. Call same-day for any of these.

What about blood clots?

Risk is elevated for several weeks after surgery and is mitigated by sequential compression devices intraop, walking starting day of surgery, and pharmacologic prophylaxis (heparin / LMWH) in higher-risk patients[5]Geerts et al., Prevention of venous thromboembolism: American College of Chest Phy..., Chest 2008 · PMID 18574271. Call immediately for: calf or thigh pain or swelling (DVT), sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or fast heart rate (PE).

What about Ozempic / GLP-1 medications around surgery?

The current anesthesia guidance[7]Oh et al., Perioperative risks of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists an..., Anesth Pain Med (Seoul) 2026 · PMID 42135216: hold daily GLP-1 preparations for at least 1 day pre-op; hold weekly preparations for at least 1 week pre-op (some societies recommend up to 2 weeks). The concern is delayed gastric emptying and aspiration risk under anesthesia. You will need to discuss timing with both your prescriber and your anesthesia team well in advance of surgery. Same applies if you’re on tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound).

When is my follow-up?

Typically 2 weeks after surgery for incision check, pathology review, removal of any vaginal packing or skin sutures, and review of activity restrictions. A second visit at 6 weeks is standard for hysterectomy or larger procedures. The portal will have your specific schedule.

    Gynecologic surgery is very common: hysterectomy alone is one of the most frequently performed operating room procedures each year. It is well known that surgical stress induces a catabolic state that leads to increased cardiac demand, relative tissue hypoxia, increased insulin resistance, impaired coagulation profiles, and altered pulmonary and gastrointestinal function. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathways were developed with the goal of maintaining normal physiology in the perioperative period, thus optimizing patient outcomes without increasing postoperative complications or readmissions. The basic principles of ERAS include attention to the following: preoperative counseling and nutritional strategies, including avoidance of prolonged perioperative fasting; perioperative considerations, including a focus on regional anesthetic and nonopioid analgesic approaches, fluid balance, and maintenance of normothermia; and promotion of postoperative recovery strategies, including early mobilization and appropriate thromboprophylaxis. Implementation of ERAS protocols has not been shown to increase readmission, mortality, or reoperation rates. These benefits have been replicated across the spectrum of gynecologic surgeries, including open and minimally invasive approaches and benign and oncologic surgeries. The implementation of the ERAS program requires collaboration from all members of the surgical team. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery is a comprehensive program, and data demonstrate success when multiple components of the ERAS pathway are implemented together. Successful ERAS pathway implementation across the spectrum of gynecologic care has the potential to improve patient care and health care delivery systems.
