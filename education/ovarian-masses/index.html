Ovarian cysts & adnexal masses — how we evaluate them, when surgery is right.

Ovarian cysts are common — most women develop them at some point, and the great majority are benign and self-resolving. But when a cyst is found on imaging, the right next steps depend on age, the cyst’s features on ultrasound, whether you have symptoms, and any family history that raises hereditary cancer risk. The categories of ovarian cysts and masses

“Ovarian cyst” is a catch-all term that covers very different entities — the right management depends on which one you actually have.

Functional / physiologic

  • Follicular cyst — a developing follicle that didn’t release its egg. Resolves over 1–3 cycles.
  • Corpus luteum cyst — the structure left behind after ovulation, sometimes filled with blood. Resolves in days to weeks.
  • Theca lutein cyst — less common, associated with elevated hCG (pregnancy, gestational trophoblastic disease).
  • All are benign. Most need no intervention beyond a follow-up ultrasound to document resolution.

Benign neoplasms

  • Dermoid (mature cystic teratoma) — benign, but doesn’t resolve. Classic mixed-density appearance on imaging.
  • Serous or mucinous cystadenoma — benign epithelial tumors. Can grow large.
  • Endometrioma — “chocolate cyst”; endometriosis on the ovary. See the endometriosis guide.
  • Fibroma / thecoma — solid benign stromal tumors. Sometimes cause Meigs syndrome (ascites + pleural effusion).
  • Hydrosalpinx — not actually an ovarian cyst; a dilated fallopian tube, often mistaken for a cyst on imaging.

A separate category is malignant or borderline ovarian tumors — epithelial ovarian cancer (the most common), borderline serous or mucinous tumors, germ cell tumors (more common in younger women), sex-cord stromal tumors. Most adnexal masses are NOT malignant, but the imaging workup is designed to identify the ones that are.

The workup

The standard pathway is designed to maximize sensitivity for malignancy while avoiding unnecessary surgery on benign disease.

  1. History. Pain (acute or chronic), pressure symptoms, abnormal bleeding, weight changes, GI symptoms (bloating, early satiety, change in bowel habits — the suspicious ones for ovarian cancer that persist over >12 days/month)[4]Committee Opinion No. 716: The Role of the Obstetrician-Gynecologis..., Obstet Gynecol 2017 · PMID 28832487, family history of ovarian / breast / colorectal cancer, age, menopausal status, prior surgery, fertility plans.
  2. Exam. Bimanual to characterize the mass — size, mobility, tenderness, consistency, bilaterality, ascites. Irregular, firm, fixed, nodular, bilateral, or associated with ascites raises malignancy concern.
  3. Transvaginal ultrasound is first-line. Characterize the mass: simple vs complex (septations, solid components, papillary projections), vascularity on Doppler, free fluid in the cul-de-sac. The IOTA Simple Rules[2]Van Calster et al., Practical guidance for applying the ADNEX model from the IOTA group..., Facts Views Vis Obgyn 2015 · PMID 25897370 use 10 ultrasound features (5 benign, 5 malignant) to risk-stratify the mass.
  4. Tumor markers, when appropriate. CA-125 in postmenopausal women with a suspicious mass; less reliable in premenopausal women because it’s also elevated by endometriosis, fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease, pregnancy, and even menstruation. CA-125 misses 50% of Stage I disease — a normal value doesn’t exclude cancer. Other markers (HE4, ROMA, AFP, beta-hCG, LDH, inhibin) used selectively based on the imaging and clinical suspicion.
  5. CT or MRI are NOT first-line. CT is useful for staging if cancer is suspected (assesses peritoneal disease, lymph nodes, distant spread). MRI sometimes helps distinguish dermoid vs endometrioma vs solid tumor when ultrasound is equivocal.
  6. Gynecologic-oncology referral for any high-risk imaging features, elevated CA-125 (or RMI/ROMA score) in a postmenopausal woman, suspicious clinical findings, or strong family history. Outcomes are better when ovarian cancer surgery is performed by a gynecologic oncologist.

Watch, image, or operate — the decision tree

Once a mass is characterized, the decision falls along a spectrum — from no follow-up needed, to interval re-imaging, to surgery.

0

No follow-up

Simple cysts ≤3 cm in premenopausal women, or simple cysts ≤1 cm in postmenopausal women, with classic features — no action needed[3]Levine et al., Management of asymptomatic ovarian and other adnexal cysts imaged a..., Radiology 2010 · PMID 20505067.

1

Re-image in 6–12 weeks

Suspected functional cyst — document resolution after 1–3 cycles. Most premenopausal cysts <5 cm resolve.

2

Re-image at 6–12 months

Stable benign-appearing mass — benign cystadenoma, small endometrioma, dermoid. Watch for growth or new features.

3

Surgery

Symptomatic (pain, pressure), growing rapidly, suspicious features on imaging, persistent cysts >5–10 cm, or fertility-impacting endometriomas. Laparoscopic when feasible[6]Liu et al., Meta-analysis comparing the safety of laparoscopic and open surgica..., Int J Gynaecol Obstet 2017 · PMID 28099685.

!

GYN-Onc referral

Suspicious imaging, elevated risk markers in postmenopausal women, or any imaging features in postmenopausal women that don’t look completely benign.

Strong family history or known BRCA mutation changes management [7]Huber et al., Hormone replacement therapy in BRCA mutation carriers and risk of o..., J Cancer Res Clin Oncol 2021 · PMID 33885953. Risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy is the most effective single intervention.

Most common patient questions

Is my cyst cancer?

Almost certainly not. Across all ages, about 80% of adnexal masses are benign — and in premenopausal women that rises to over 90%. The workup is designed to pick out the small fraction that need cancer evaluation. The most reassuring features on ultrasound: simple appearance (thin-walled, anechoic interior), <5 cm, no septations, no solid components, no abnormal Doppler signal[2]Van Calster et al., Practical guidance for applying the ADNEX model from the IOTA group..., Facts Views Vis Obgyn 2015 · PMID 25897370.

Why didn’t we just send a CA-125?

CA-125 is useful in some settings but unhelpful in others. It’s elevated in 80% of epithelial ovarian cancers overall but misses 50% of Stage I disease. In premenopausal women it’s also elevated by endometriosis, fibroids, PID, pregnancy, even menstruation — producing false positives that lead to unnecessary anxiety and surgery. The marker is most useful in postmenopausal women with a suspicious mass, not as a general screening tool.

Why don’t we screen all women for ovarian cancer?

Because no validated screening tool reduces ovarian cancer mortality in average-risk women[4]Committee Opinion No. 716: The Role of the Obstetrician-Gynecologis..., Obstet Gynecol 2017 · PMID 28832487[5]Kahn et al., Salpingectomy for the Primary Prevention of Ovarian Cancer: A Syste..., JAMA Surg 2023 · PMID 37672283. Combinations of TVUS and CA-125 in large randomized trials have failed to show survival benefit. Symptom recognition (persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic or abdominal pain, urinary urgency for more than ~12 days a month) is the current best alerting system, alongside individual evaluation when a mass is identified for any other reason.

Will I lose my ovary?

When surgery is needed, the question is whether to do an ovarian cystectomy (remove the cyst, preserve the ovary) or an oophorectomy (remove the ovary). The answer depends on age, fertility plans, the size and nature of the lesion, and whether there’s suspicion for malignancy. In premenopausal women with benign lesions, cystectomy is preferred whenever feasible to preserve ovarian function. In postmenopausal women, oophorectomy may be appropriate, particularly with suspicious features.

Open or laparoscopic?

For benign-appearing masses, laparoscopy is the standard[6]Liu et al., Meta-analysis comparing the safety of laparoscopic and open surgica..., Int J Gynaecol Obstet 2017 · PMID 28099685 — less blood loss, less pain, shorter stay, faster recovery, smaller scars. Open surgery is reserved for very large masses, strong suspicion of malignancy (where intact removal in a bag matters to avoid spread), or when the patient has anatomy that makes laparoscopy unsafe. Robotic-assisted laparoscopy is an option for complex cases.

Should I get BRCA tested?

If your family history includes ovarian, fallopian-tube, or peritoneal cancer at any age, or breast cancer (especially under 50, bilateral, triple-negative, or male breast cancer), or if you’re of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry (founder mutation frequency ~1 in 40) — yes, BRCA testing should be discussed[7]Huber et al., Hormone replacement therapy in BRCA mutation carriers and risk of o..., J Cancer Res Clin Oncol 2021 · PMID 33885953. The result changes management of any current adnexal mass, informs prophylactic risk-reduction options (risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy, intensified breast screening), and matters for your relatives.

What if the cyst has gotten larger?

Growth raises the threshold for surgery. Functional cysts should resolve, not grow. Stable benign cystadenomas or dermoids that increase in size, new solid or septated components, or rising tumor markers all push toward surgical evaluation. Imaging at a defined interval (3–6 months) is how growth is documented.

    Ovarian cancer is the second most common type of female reproductive cancer, and more women die from ovarian cancer than from cervical cancer and uterine cancer combined. Currently, there is no strategy for early detection of ovarian cancer that reduces ovarian cancer mortality.     PMID 37672283
    Read the abstract
    IMPORTANCE: Most ovarian cancers originate in the fimbriated end of the fallopian tube. This has led to the hypothesis that surgical resection of the fallopian tubes at the time of gynecologic and nongynecologic surgical procedures-referred to as an opportunistic salpingectomy-may prevent the development of epithelial ovarian cancer for women at an average risk of developing the disease.

    OBJECTIVE: To compile a comprehensive, state-of-the-science review examining the current landscape of performing bilateral salpingectomy for ovarian cancer prevention.

    EVIDENCE REVIEW: A systematic review of the literature was performed on March 4, 2022, to identify studies examining salpingectomy for ovarian cancer prevention. This review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. Four databases were selected: PubMed via the National Library of Medicine's PubMed.gov, Embase via Elsevier's Embase.com, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) via Wiley's Cochrane Library, and Northern Light Life Sciences Conference Abstracts via Ovid. A total of 20 gray literature sources, including 1 database, 2 registers, 1 repository, 1 index, 1 archive, 1 preprint server, 1 agency, and 12 organizations, were also searched.

    FINDINGS: The initial search produced 1089 results; a total of 158 publications were included in the final review. Salpingectomy has been associated with ovarian cancer risk reduction of approximately 80%. Studies have demonstrated that salpingectomy was safe, cost-effective, and was not associated with an earlier age of menopause onset. With widespread implementation, salpingectomy has the potential to reduce ovarian cancer mortality in the US by an estimated 15%. Both physician and patient awareness regarding the adnexa as the origin for most ovarian cancers, as well as the existence of salpingectomy and its potential benefits in reducing ovarian cancer risk, has increased during the past decade. Raising awareness and developing effective implementation strategies are essential.

    CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE: The results of this systematic review suggest that bilateral salpingectomy for ovarian cancer prevention was safe and feasible and has the potential to be a cost-effective and cost-saving strategy across the population. Prospective studies to demonstrate long-term survival outcomes and feasibility in nongynecologic surgical procedures are warranted.

    BACKGROUND: The safety of laparoscopic surgery during the second trimester of pregnancy remains a controversial subject.

    OBJECTIVES: To compare the safety of laparoscopic surgery and laparotomy for suspected adnexal mass during the second trimester.

    SEARCH STRATEGY: Articles published in any language prior to April 31, 2016, were retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, EMBSCO, and the Cochrane Library using keywords including pregnant, adnexal mass, laparoscopy, laparotomy, pregnancy outcomes, and surgical outcomes.

    SELECTION CRITERIA: Randomized and non-randomized controlled trials reporting at least one obstetric or surgical outcome were included if they compared laparoscopic surgery and laparotomy for adnexal masses during the second trimester.

    DATA COLLECTION AND ANALYSIS: Data were independently extracted by two reviewers. Homogeneous data were pooled using a fixed effects model and heterogeneous data were qualitatively analyzed.
