Fellowship in Minimally Invasive Gynecologic Surgery (FMIGS) — Curriculum and Instructional Design

Abstract

This curriculum design presents a comprehensive curriculum for the Fellowship in Minimally Invasive Gynecologic Surgery (FMIGS) program at PRIME Illinois St Francis Hospital in Evanston Illinois and Metro Chicagoland area. The curriculum aligns with the program requirements set forth by the FMIGS Board and incorporates evidence-based approaches to ensure that fellows acquire advanced knowledge and surgical skills in minimally invasive gynecologic surgery (MIGS) per AAGL FMIGS program requirements and learning objectives. The curriculum integrates principles of adult learning theory using an instructional design and curriculum approach to enhance educational effectiveness, ensuring that learning experiences are self-directed, experiential, goal-oriented, and relevant to the fellows' professional practice (Knowles, Holton, & Swanson, 2015; Dick and Carey 2011). Detailed components such as context analysis, needs assessment, instructional plans, and evaluation strategies are provided to prepare fellows to serve as independent specialists and leaders in MIGS, addressing the healthcare needs, health equity and disparities impacting underserved populations and advancing the field through excellence in complex surgical management.

Chapter 2

Introduction

Minimally invasive gynecologic surgery (MIGS) has transformed women’s healthcare by offering surgical solutions that reduce patient morbidity, improve recovery times, and enhance overall quality of life (Nieboer et al., 2009). However, disparities in access to such advanced surgical care persist, particularly among low-income and underserved communities (Jacoby et al., 2011). Recent studies underscore the ongoing challenges in achieving diversity within MIGS fellowships and highlight systemic barriers that contribute to unequal access to minimally invasive surgical services (Hamilton et al., 2024; Isa et al., 2024; Sweterlitsch et al., 2024; Delara et al., 2020).

This FMIGS curriculum is specifically designed to train gynecologic surgeons who are committed to community-based care and the equitable healthcare delivery of MIGS and benign-complex disease management. By emphasizing the unique challenges and needs of low-income populations, the program aims to reduce healthcare disparities and improve outcomes for these communities (Williams et al., 2010).

Studies have shown that racial and ethnic minorities often experience higher rates of complications and readmissions after gynecologic surgery. For instance, Black women have been found to have a 30% higher risk of complications compared to White women in some studies. Minority women are less likely to receive minimally invasive procedures, which are associated with shorter hospital stays and faster recovery times. For example, Black and Hispanic women are up to 40% less likely to receive minimally invasive hysterectomies compared to their White counterparts. Some data suggest that Black women have a higher mortality rate following gynecologic surgery, which can be attributed to a combination of factors, including later-stage diagnosis and limited access to high-quality care.

The curriculum addresses these disparities by fostering a diverse cohort of fellows and promoting inclusive referral practices, as highlighted by recent literature identifying the importance of diversity and equitable training environments (Hamilton et al., 2024; Delara et al., 2020).

Research shows that women from low-income backgrounds have less access to MIGS. This disparity often leads to delayed care, progression of gynecologic conditions, and poorer outcomes compared to those with greater means. Black and Hispanic women face additional barriers, such as lower referral rates for MIGS and limited insurance coverage. Implicit biases within the healthcare system can also impact the quality of care and the surgical options offered to these women, resulting in fewer minimally invasive procedures and more complications.

To address these inequities, some low-income or community-based areas have developed targeted initiatives. These include expanding community health programs, enhancing the training of local healthcare providers in MIGS, and utilizing telemedicine to reach underserved populations. However, challenges such as limited funding and infrastructure persist, requiring continued advocacy and innovation.

Simultaneously, the fellowship is dedicated to advancing the field of MIGS by fostering and mentoring leaders with specialized knowledge in complex benign pathology and diseases, and expertise in the minimally invasive surgical management of these conditions (Advincula & Wang, 2009). Evidence from recent studies demonstrates that specialized MIGS training is associated with improved surgical outcomes and reduced complications, reinforcing the value of advanced training programs (Meyer et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2022; Pfeuti et al., 2023).

The curriculum incorporates adult learning principles to ensure that educational experiences are relevant, practical, and engaging for adult learners (Knowles et al., 2015). By promoting self-directed learning, leveraging fellows’ prior experiences, and focusing on problem-solving and application, the program enhances the effectiveness of the learning process (Taylor & Hamdy, 2013). Furthermore, the program integrates simulation-based training and surgical intelligence tools, aligning with current trends in MIGS education that emphasize hands-on, technology-enhanced learning to improve surgical proficiency and patient outcomes (Hamilton et al., 2024; Pando et al., [year]; Levin et al., 2024).

Additionally, the curriculum addresses compensation trends and career development, ensuring that graduates are well-prepared for sustainable careers in MIGS. By understanding current compensation structures and addressing barriers to fellowship pursuit, the program supports fellows in achieving professional and financial success (Adedayo et al., 2024; Dassel et al., 2015; Traylor et al., 2019).

By addressing both the educational needs of fellows and the broader systemic issues within MIGS, the FMIGS curriculum strives to produce competent, compassionate, and socially responsible gynecologic surgeons. These surgeons are equipped to lead advancements in minimally invasive surgery, promote health equity, and improve patient outcomes across diverse communities.

This analysis facilitates a tailored, competency-based approach that maximizes learning outcomes while being cost-effective and resource-conscious. By addressing the unique health needs of the community, available institutional resources, and adult learning principles, the curriculum becomes more relevant, impactful, and designed to produce highly skilled, well-rounded, and compassionate minimally invasive gynecologic surgeons.

Chapter 5

Needs Assessment

A comprehensive needs assessment was conducted using data from local health departments, community health surveys, and national health statistics to identify gaps in gynecologic surgical care and the educational needs of the fellows.

Community Needs

The FMIGS fellowship program is dedicated to addressing the unique health needs and disparities present within the diverse communities of Evanston and the urban Chicago-land area. This commitment is underscored by the high prevalence of gynecologic conditions, limited access to advanced surgical care, cultural and language barriers, and health disparities that necessitate specialized training and targeted interventions. Incorporating specific demographic and diagnostic data from reliable sources emphasizes the program’s relevance and necessity.

High Prevalence of Gynecologic Conditions

Uterine Fibroids: In Evanston, African American women experience a higher prevalence of uterine fibroids compared to other ethnic groups, leading to increased morbidity and a greater need for surgical intervention (Baird et al., 2003). Studies have shown that Black women are three times more likely to develop fibroids than White women, often at a younger age and with more severe symptoms (Stewart et al., 2013). The FMIGS curriculum must equip fellows with specialized surgical approaches, including minimally invasive hysterectomies and myomectomies, to manage these complex cases effectively.

  • In Chicago, Hispanic women also exhibit a significant prevalence of fibroids, necessitating specialized surgical approaches to manage larger and more symptomatic fibroids effectively (Stewart et al., 2017). This underscores the importance of advanced MIGS training to address these challenges and improve patient outcomes in these communities.

Endometriosis and Chronic Pelvic Pain: In both Evanston and Chicago, up to 10% of women of reproductive age are affected by endometriosis and chronic pelvic pain—conditions that often require advanced surgical management to alleviate symptoms and improve quality of life (Bulun, 2009). Effective treatment of these conditions is essential for reducing long-term health complications and enhancing patient well-being. Advanced laparoscopic techniques taught in the FMIGS program are crucial for accurate diagnosis and effective management (Falcone & Flyckt, 2018).

Limited Access to Advanced Surgical Care

Underutilization of Minimally Invasive Surgery (MIS): Women from low-income and minority backgrounds in Evanston and Chicago are less likely to receive MIS procedures such as laparoscopic hysterectomies compared to their higher-income and White counterparts (Anderson et al., 2013). This underutilization results in longer recovery times, higher complication rates, and increased healthcare costs. A study by Johnson et al. (2017) found that minority women were 40% less likely to undergo minimally invasive hysterectomies. The FMIGS fellowship aims to bridge this gap by training surgeons in advanced MIS techniques and promoting their adoption within local healthcare facilities.

Insurance and Financial Barriers: St. Francis Hospital serves as one of the few safety-net hospitals in Chicago, catering to a substantial population of uninsured (30%) and underinsured (50%) patients (Illinois Department of Healthcare and Family Services, 2019). These patients face significant obstacles in accessing specialized surgical care, including MIS procedures, due to financial constraints and limited insurance coverage. Financial barriers often delay necessary treatments, leading to worsened health outcomes and exacerbating healthcare disparities (Artiga et al., 2019). The FMIGS fellowship program incorporates comprehensive training on navigating insurance complexities and advocating for policy reforms that expand coverage for minimally invasive procedures.

Cultural and Language Barriers

Health Literacy Issues: Approximately 25% of patients in Evanston and Chicago communities have limited health literacy, contributing to misunderstandings about gynecologic conditions and treatments (McCaffery et al., 2016). This barrier impedes effective communication between patients and healthcare providers, leading to decreased adherence to treatment plans and poorer health outcomes. Enhancing health literacy is essential for empowering patients to make informed healthcare decisions. The FMIGS curriculum includes training on communicating complex medical information in understandable ways to improve patient engagement and outcomes.

Language Barriers: Over 40% of the patient population in Chicago’s underserved communities are non-English speakers, primarily immigrants from Hispanic and Asian backgrounds (U.S. Census Bureau, 2020). These language barriers negatively impact the quality of care, as patients may struggle to communicate symptoms, understand treatment plans, and follow postoperative instructions (Flores, 2006). Providing language-appropriate resources, including trained medical interpreters and translated educational materials, is critical for ensuring equitable access to healthcare. The fellowship program incorporates training on cultural competence and the effective use of interpreter services to overcome these barriers (Betancourt et al., 2016).

Health Disparities and Outcomes

Disparities in Cancer Screening: Minority women in Evanston and Chicago have lower rates of cervical cancer screening and higher mortality rates due to late-stage diagnosis (Benard et al., 2014). African American and Hispanic women are more likely to be diagnosed with advanced-stage cervical cancer, resulting in poorer prognoses (Rositch et al., 2014). This disparity highlights the need for outreach and education programs to improve screening rates. The FMIGS curriculum emphasizes the importance of proactive screening and early detection, training fellows to implement and advocate for community-based screening initiatives.

Delayed Care Seeking: Cultural beliefs and mistrust in the healthcare system contribute to delays in seeking care, especially among underserved populations in Evanston and Chicago (Jacobs et al., 2014). Historical injustices and ongoing discrimination have led to skepticism towards medical institutions (Scharff et al., 2010). Addressing these factors through community engagement, culturally sensitive care, and building trust can improve timely access to necessary treatments. The fellowship program includes training on culturally sensitive communication, community outreach strategies, and methods to build trust with patients.

Fellows’ Needs

To effectively address the community needs outlined above, the FMIGS curriculum must cater to the comprehensive training requirements of its fellows, including advanced surgical skills, cultural competence, research capabilities, and professional development.

Proficiency in MIGS Techniques

Fellows require extensive training in advanced laparoscopic and robotic surgeries to manage complex gynecologic cases effectively (Advincula & Wang, 2009). Mastery of these techniques is essential for minimizing surgical complications and improving patient outcomes, particularly in cases involving uterine fibroids and endometriosis prevalent in the communities served.

Exposure to a Diverse Case Mix

Training across various clinical settings with a wide range of gynecologic conditions enhances surgical competence and prepares fellows to handle diverse patient populations (Azziz et al., 2009). Exposure to different case types fosters adaptability and comprehensive clinical expertise.

Cultural Competence Training

Effective Communication Skills: The ability to communicate effectively with patients from diverse cultural backgrounds improves patient satisfaction and adherence to treatment plans (Betancourt et al., 2016). Training in cultural competence ensures that fellows can provide respectful and individualized care, addressing the unique cultural and linguistic needs of patients in Evanston and Chicago.

Understanding Social Determinants of Health: Recognizing the impact of socioeconomic factors on health outcomes allows fellows to develop more comprehensive patient care plans (Artiga & Hinton, 2018). This understanding is crucial for addressing the broader context of patients’ health and improving overall care quality, particularly in underserved communities.

Research Skills

Evidence-Based Practice: Training in research methodologies enables fellows to contribute to advancements in MIGS and address health disparities through evidence-based interventions (Sullivan, 2011). Engaging in research fosters critical thinking and continuous improvement in clinical practices.

Focus on Health Disparities: Encouraging fellows to engage in research that explores and seeks to reduce disparities can lead to improved care for underserved populations (Braveman et al., 2011). This focus aligns with the program’s commitment to health equity and social responsibility.

Professional Development

Leadership Training: Developing leadership skills prepares fellows to advocate for patients and influence healthcare policies effectively (Blumenthal et al., 2012). Leadership training empowers fellows to drive positive changes within their institutions and the broader healthcare system.

Teaching Skills: The ability to educate residents, medical students, and patients fosters the next generation of healthcare providers and promotes informed communities (Skeff et al., 2010). Teaching skills are essential for fellows to disseminate knowledge and best practices in MIGS.

Barriers to Transfer of Learning

Identifying barriers to the transfer of learning from educational settings to clinical practice is essential for developing effective strategies to overcome them. Within the context of urban, diverse, and underserved healthcare environments, these barriers can be categorized into individual, organizational, and cultural/language-related challenges. Understanding and addressing these barriers will enhance the effectiveness of the FMIGS fellowship program and ensure that fellows can seamlessly integrate their acquired skills into clinical practice.

Individual Barriers

Time Constraints

Heavy Clinical Workload: Fellows often juggle demanding clinical responsibilities alongside their educational activities. This dual burden can limit opportunities for focused learning and skill development, hindering the acquisition of advanced MIGS techniques (Lefebvre et al., 2016). Implementing structured scheduling and prioritizing educational activities within the fellowship program are necessary to mitigate this barrier. The FMIGS program adheres to the ACGME guideline that state “clinical and educational work hours must be limited to no more than 80 hours per week, averaged over a four-week period, inclusive of all in-house clinical and educational activities, clinical work done from home, and all moonlighting.

Risk of Burnout: High levels of stress and fatigue are prevalent among fellows due to the intense nature of clinical duties combined with rigorous training requirements. Burnout can impair learning, reduce performance, and negatively impact overall well-being (Dyrbye et al., 2017). Incorporating wellness programs and providing mental health support can help alleviate burnout risks.

Skill Acquisition Challenges

Steep Learning Curve: Advanced MIGS techniques require significant practice and repetition. Inadequate exposure to these techniques within clinical rotations can hinder skill mastery (Donnellan et al., 2017). The FMIGS curriculum must ensure ample hands-on training opportunities through simulation labs and supervised surgical procedures to support skill acquisition.

Confidence Levels: Fellows may experience a lack of confidence in applying new skills in real-world clinical settings, especially when dealing with complex cases prevalent in diverse and underserved communities (Bandura, 1997). Building confidence through incremental learning, mentorship, and supportive feedback is crucial for effective skill transfer.

Organizational Barriers

Resource Limitations

Limited Access to Simulation, Cadaver Labs, Facilities: Although some institutions provide state-of-the-art surgical facilities, there may still be limitations in simulation resources specific to MIGS training (Stefanidis et al., 2013). Enhancing simulation-based training infrastructure is essential to provide fellows with the necessary environment to practice and refine their surgical skills.

Financial Constraints: Budget limitations in community hospital programs can restrict access to essential educational materials, conferences, and advanced training opportunities (Curet et al., 2017). Securing funding through grants, partnerships, and institutional support is vital to ensure that fellows have access to comprehensive educational resources.

Systemic Challenges

Complex Healthcare Systems: Navigating intricate healthcare systems can pose challenges for fellows in implementing learned practices effectively (Woolf et al., 2015). Training on healthcare system navigation, including understanding referral processes and interdisciplinary collaboration, can enhance fellows' ability to apply their skills efficiently.

Resistance to Change: Institutional culture and established practices may resist the adoption of new approaches or technologies, impeding the integration of advanced MIGS techniques (Grol et al., 2013). Promoting a culture of continuous improvement and encouraging innovation within healthcare settings can help overcome resistance to change.

Cultural and Language Barriers

Communication Gaps

Language Differences: In underserved urban communities, a significant portion of patients are non-English speakers, primarily immigrants from diverse backgrounds. Communication difficulties can impede effective care delivery, making it challenging for fellows to apply their MIGS skills in a culturally competent manner (Diamond et al., 2019). Providing comprehensive language support services, including trained medical interpreters and multilingual educational materials, is essential for ensuring equitable care.

Cultural Misunderstandings: Lack of cultural awareness among fellows may lead to misinterpretation of patient needs and preferences, affecting the quality of care (Betancourt et al., 2016). Incorporating cultural competence training into the FMIGS curriculum will enable fellows to better understand and respect the diverse cultural backgrounds of their patients, fostering more effective patient-provider interactions.

Patient Trust and Engagement

Mistrust in Healthcare Providers: Historical injustices and perceived discrimination contribute to mistrust among minority populations in urban settings (Cuevas et al., 2016). This mistrust can lead to reluctance in seeking care and adhering to treatment plans. Training fellows in building trust through compassionate, respectful, and transparent communication is crucial for improving patient engagement and outcomes.

Health Literacy: Low health literacy affects patient understanding and adherence to treatment plans, especially in communities with diverse educational backgrounds (McCaffery et al., 2016). Fellows must be trained to assess and address health literacy levels, utilizing clear communication strategies and educational tools to enhance patient comprehension and involvement in their care. Fellows will begin to integrate social determinants of health (SDH) interviewing to assess for these barriers during history and intake.

Strategies to Improve Transfer of Learning

The FMIGS fellowship curriculum has been specifically designed to address barriers to the transfer of learning from educational settings to clinical practice. By integrating evidence-based strategies targeting individual, organizational, and cultural challenges within diverse and underserved urban healthcare environments, the program ensures that fellows are equipped to effectively apply their knowledge and skills in clinical practice. This approach aims to improve patient outcomes and reduce health disparities (Wong et al., 2018).

Enhancing Individual Learning

Dedicated Educational Blocks

Dedicated time blocks for education are built into the weekly schedule, ensuring that learning activities such as lectures, workshops, and case discussions are prioritized without interference from clinical duties. Structuring these sessions into rotation schedules allows for consistent and predictable learning opportunities. An annual educational calendar is distributed to fellows, outlining all scheduled activities to facilitate advanced planning alongside clinical responsibilities (Owen et al., 2018).

Enforcement of Work-Hour Policies

The program adheres strictly to duty-hour regulations to ensure that fellows have adequate rest and do not experience excessive workloads. Mandatory rest periods and time-off policies are enforced to mitigate burnout and enhance learning capacity. Studies have shown that compliance with duty-hour policies improves resident well-being without compromising patient care (Bolster & Rourke, 2015). Flexible scheduling options, including adjusted shifts and part-time rotations, are available to accommodate individual needs and promote a healthy work-life balance (Goitein et al., 2015).

Simulation-Based Training

High-Fidelity Simulation Technology

The curriculum incorporates advanced high-fidelity simulation training, providing fellows with access to state-of-the-art simulators that replicate real-life surgical scenarios. Regular simulation sessions allow fellows to continuously practice and refine their minimally invasive gynecologic surgery (MIGS) techniques in a controlled environment. Simulation-based education has been shown to enhance surgical skills and improve patient safety outcomes (Cook et al., 2011).

Structured Practice Sessions

Structured practice sessions are embedded in the curriculum, enabling fellows to perform specific surgical tasks under direct faculty supervision with immediate feedback. These sessions include hands-on workshops and cadaver dissections seminars offered nationally, offering practical, supported settings for skill development. Deliberate practice with feedback is essential for acquiring expert performance in surgical skills (Ericsson, 2004). Attendance of at least one specialized workshops over course of fellowship, such as those offered at national conferences, is required to ensure comprehensive skill acquisition.

Mentorship and Coaching

Personalized Faculty Mentorship

Key Adult Learning Theories Integrated into the Curriculum:

Adult Learning Theory (Andragogy)

This theory emphasizes that adults learn best when they are involved in planning their learning and when the learning is relevant to their experiences and needs. According to Knowles (1984), adult learning is characterized by self-directed learning, where learners take the initiative in diagnosing their learning needs, formulating goals, identifying resources, and evaluating outcomes. Knowles (1984) also emphasizes that adults bring a wealth of life experience that they integrate into their learning process, using these experiences as a foundation for absorbing new knowledge. Moreover, adults tend to be motivated by the practical application of knowledge, especially when it relates directly to solving real-life problems or improving their personal and professional lives (Knowles, 1984). The FMIGS curriculum incorporates self-directed learning, problem-solving exercises, and interactive case-based teaching strategies to optimize adult learning outcomes.

Experiential Learning Theory (Kolb)

This theory posits that learning occurs through experience and reflection. In medical education, this would manifest in case-based learning, simulation activities, and hands-on clinical practice. Kolb’s (1984) experiential learning theory argues that learning is a continuous process grounded in experience. Kolb (1984) outlines a four-stage learning cycle: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. Learners move through these stages by first having a direct experience, reflecting on it, conceptualizing what they have learned, and then applying this new knowledge in practical scenarios. According to Kolb (1984), this process is iterative and builds upon the learner’s previous experiences. Simulation training, surgical video review, and post-operative debriefing sessions follow this model, reinforcing skill acquisition through direct experience and reflection.

Constructivism

This theory focuses on learners constructing their own understanding and knowledge of the world through experiences and reflecting on those experiences. Constructivist learning theory posits that individuals actively construct knowledge by interacting with their environment and building on prior knowledge. Piaget (1972) and Vygotsky (1978) both contributed to the understanding of constructivism, emphasizing that learners actively create their understanding based on personal experiences and that learning is a social process influenced by interactions with others. This theory encourages learners to become active participants in their learning rather than passive recipients of information (Piaget, 1972; Vygotsky, 1978). The FMIGS program applies this by integrating problem-based learning, surgical case discussions, and research-focused inquiry to encourage critical thinking.

Transformational Learning

This theory explains how critical reflection on one’s beliefs and experiences can lead to a profound change in perspective, which is important in fields like palliative care where end-of-life considerations challenge many existing beliefs. Mezirow’s (1991) theory of transformational learning describes how adults can experience deep shifts in their worldview through critical reflection. According to Mezirow (1991), transformational learning occurs when individuals critically examine their assumptions and beliefs, leading to a fundamental change in their perspectives and behaviors. This theory highlights the importance of discourse and reflection in fostering transformative learning experiences that can significantly alter an individual’s approach to life and work (Mezirow, 1991). This is applied in structured feedback sessions, ethical decision-making workshops, and patient-centered care discussions.

Social Learning Theory

Emphasizing learning through observation and interaction with others, this theory may be used in settings like interdisciplinary team-based care in hospice education. Bandura’s (1977) social learning theory emphasizes the role of observational learning, imitation, and modeling in behavior acquisition. According to Bandura (1977), individuals learn by observing the actions of others and the consequences that follow, integrating these observations into their behavior. This theory underscores the significance of social interaction and environmental factors in shaping an individual’s learning processes and behaviors. Fellows will participate in interdisciplinary surgical teams, observational learning through mentor shadowing, and peer-assisted learning models.

The package includes both formative and summative assessments, utilizing a variety of evaluation tools to ensure fellows achieve competency in all required areas.
