What counts as “abnormal”?

A normal menstrual cycle, by current consensus, has a frequency of 24–38 days, a duration of 4.5–8 days, and a per-cycle blood loss of 5–80 mL[2]Munro et al., The two FIGO systems for normal and abnormal uterine bleeding sympt..., Int J Gynaecol Obstet 2018 · PMID 30198563. Anything outside those ranges — or any bleeding between cycles, after intercourse, or after menopause — counts as abnormal and deserves an evaluation.

In practical terms, the patterns that bring most women in are:

  • Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) — soaking through a pad or tampon every 1–2 hours, passing clots larger than a quarter, flooding through clothes or bedding, requiring double protection, or bleeding for more than 7 days. 10–30% of reproductive-age women experience HMB at some point[4]Bianchi et al., Utility of the Levonorgestrel-Releasing Intrauterine System in the ..., J Clin Med 2022 · PMID 36233703.
  • Bleeding between periods (intermenstrual) — any spotting or bleeding outside the expected window. Added to the formal definition in 2018[2]Munro et al., The two FIGO systems for normal and abnormal uterine bleeding sympt..., Int J Gynaecol Obstet 2018 · PMID 30198563.
  • Bleeding after sex (postcoital) — needs evaluation for cervical pathology (polyp, ectropion, infection, dysplasia, rarely cancer).
  • Postmenopausal bleeding — any bleeding after 12 months of amenorrhea in a menopausal woman. Every episode is evaluated, because PMB is the cardinal sign of endometrial cancer[3]Narice et al., Endometrial sampling in low-risk patients with abnormal uterine ble..., BMC Fam Pract 2018 · PMID 30060741.
  • Acute heavy bleeding — bleeding heavy enough to cause hemodynamic concerns, anemia, or require ER evaluation. Different workup, different speed.

The first thing every workup confirms is whether the patient is pregnant — pregnancy-related bleeding (miscarriage, ectopic, retained products, placental abnormalities) has its own pathway and must be excluded before any other workup proceeds[3]Narice et al., Endometrial sampling in low-risk patients with abnormal uterine ble..., BMC Fam Pract 2018 · PMID 30060741.

The 8 causes — PALM–COEIN

Since 2011, the worldwide standard for classifying causes of AUB has been the FIGO PALM–COEIN system[1]Munro et al., FIGO classification system (PALM-COEIN) for causes of abnormal uter..., Int J Gynaecol Obstet 2011 · PMID 21345435. The two halves of the acronym separate the structural causes (things you can see on imaging or biopsy) from the non-structural causes (things you can’t):

PALM — structural

  • Polyp — endometrial or endocervical, often a focal cause of intermenstrual or postmenopausal bleeding.
  • Adenomyosis — endometrial glands within the myometrium; causes heavy, painful periods and a globally enlarged uterus.
  • Leiomyoma (fibroid) — benign smooth-muscle tumors. Submucosal fibroids cause the most bleeding for their size.
  • Malignancy & hyperplasia — endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia (EIN), endometrial cancer, cervical cancer.

COEIN — non-structural

  • Coagulopathy — von Willebrand disease, platelet dysfunction, anticoagulant use. Up to 20% of HMB.
  • Ovulatory dysfunction — the leading non-structural cause; common in adolescents, perimenopause, PCOS, thyroid disease.
  • Endometrial — primary endometrial-hemostasis issue, often a diagnosis of exclusion once other causes are ruled out.
  • Iatrogenic — bleeding caused by hormones, IUDs, anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, missed doses.
  • Not otherwise classified — rare causes (arteriovenous malformation, scar pregnancy) that don’t fit anywhere else.

The reason this matters: treatment depends on the cause. A submucosal fibroid in the middle of the cavity is fixed in 20 minutes hysteroscopically; an ovulatory dysfunction in a perimenopausal woman is fixed by hormonal regulation; a coagulopathy gets the woman to a hematologist alongside management of the bleeding. Without the framework, “heavy periods” gets a blunt-instrument hysterectomy that may not have been needed.

The workup

The evaluation is layered — cheapest and least invasive first, escalating only as needed:

  1. History. Pattern (cyclic / irregular / continuous / postcoital / postmenopausal), duration, severity (pads-per-hour, clots, flooding, work/school missed), age, medications including anticoagulants and hormones, family bleeding history, prior pregnancies and procedures, weight changes, hair changes (suggesting PCOS), galactorrhea (prolactinoma).
  2. Exam. Speculum to visualize the cervix and vagina (rule out polyp, ectropion, dysplasia, trauma, infection); bimanual to assess uterine size, shape, mobility, and tenderness.
  3. Labs. Pregnancy test (always)[3]Narice et al., Endometrial sampling in low-risk patients with abnormal uterine ble..., BMC Fam Pract 2018 · PMID 30060741. CBC for anemia. TSH and prolactin for ovulatory dysfunction. von Willebrand panel + PT/PTT/platelets if a personal or family bleeding history, an adolescent with severe HMB, or HMB since menarche[3]Narice et al., Endometrial sampling in low-risk patients with abnormal uterine ble..., BMC Fam Pract 2018 · PMID 30060741.
  4. Transvaginal ultrasound. The first-line imaging study — cheap, fast, identifies fibroids, polyps, adenomyosis, endometrial thickness, ovarian pathology[3]Narice et al., Endometrial sampling in low-risk patients with abnormal uterine ble..., BMC Fam Pract 2018 · PMID 30060741.
  5. Saline-infusion sonohysterography (SIS). If TVUS suggests a focal cavity finding, SIS distends the cavity with saline so polyps, submucosal fibroids, and synechiae become unmistakable[3]Narice et al., Endometrial sampling in low-risk patients with abnormal uterine ble..., BMC Fam Pract 2018 · PMID 30060741. More sensitive than TVUS alone for intracavitary lesions.
  6. Endometrial biopsy. Required in every woman aged 45 or older with AUB, and in younger women with risk factors for hyperplasia or cancer (chronic anovulation, obesity, tamoxifen, Lynch syndrome). Postmenopausal bleeding always gets a biopsy[3]Narice et al., Endometrial sampling in low-risk patients with abnormal uterine ble..., BMC Fam Pract 2018 · PMID 30060741.
  7. Hysteroscopy. The gold standard for cavity visualization — if SIS shows a focal lesion, diagnostic hysteroscopy can be combined with operative removal in the same setting.

Most common patient questions

How heavy is “too heavy”?

The medical definition is more than 80 mL of blood loss per cycle, but nobody measures that. In real life: soaking through a pad or tampon every 1–2 hours, passing clots larger than a quarter, flooding through clothes or bedding overnight, needing double protection, or bleeding for more than 7 days — any of those qualifies as heavy menstrual bleeding and is worth a workup[4]Bianchi et al., Utility of the Levonorgestrel-Releasing Intrauterine System in the ..., J Clin Med 2022 · PMID 36233703.

Do I need to be evaluated if I bleed between periods?

Yes. Intermenstrual bleeding was added to the formal definition of AUB in 2018[2]Munro et al., The two FIGO systems for normal and abnormal uterine bleeding sympt..., Int J Gynaecol Obstet 2018 · PMID 30198563 because it often points to a focal cause (endometrial polyp, submucosal fibroid, cervical lesion) that’s easy to identify and fix.

Is the levonorgestrel IUD really that effective?

Yes — Cochrane data show menstrual blood loss reductions of 71–95% versus placebo, more effective than oral progestogens, and roughly equivalent at 1 year to endometrial ablation for satisfaction without the procedural risk[4]Bianchi et al., Utility of the Levonorgestrel-Releasing Intrauterine System in the ..., J Clin Med 2022 · PMID 36233703. For most women with HMB and no contraindication, it’s the first medical option offered.

Can I just take birth control pills?

Yes, and they work well — combined oral contraceptives reduce menstrual blood loss by 40–50% and regulate cycle pattern. Continuous use (skipping the placebo week) eliminates withdrawal bleeding altogether. The catch is the safety profile must fit — smokers over 35, women with migraine with aura, and certain clotting-risk profiles need to use a different option.

What is tranexamic acid?

A non-hormonal medication that helps blood clot inside the uterus during a heavy period. Taken only during the bleeding days (typically 1300 mg three times daily for up to 5 days), it reduces menstrual blood loss by about 40%[6]Matteson et al., Nonsurgical management of heavy menstrual bleeding: a systematic re..., Obstet Gynecol 2013 · PMID 23635628 without affecting cycle pattern, ovulation, or fertility. A great fit for women who can’t or don’t want hormones, or who only need help on the heaviest days.

Is bleeding after menopause always cancer?

No — most postmenopausal bleeding is due to a benign cause like endometrial atrophy or a polyp. But because PMB is the cardinal sign of endometrial cancer, every episode must be evaluated, even if it’s a single drop of blood on one day. The workup is straightforward: pelvic exam, TVUS for endometrial thickness, and an endometrial biopsy if indicated.

Will I need a hysterectomy?

Almost never. The modern AUB toolkit — LNG-IUD, tranexamic acid, hysteroscopic polypectomy or myomectomy, endometrial ablation — resolves the bleeding for the great majority of women without removing the uterus. Hysterectomy is reserved for cases where conservative options have failed, the uterus itself is the structural problem (large symptomatic fibroids, adenomyosis with severe pain), or there’s confirmed precancer or cancer.
