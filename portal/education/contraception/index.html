Contraception — the full menu, what fits your life.

There are more effective, well-tolerated contraceptive options today than at any point in medical history. The choice belongs to you — what matters depends on your life stage, your medical history, your fertility plans, your tolerance for side effects, and your preferences about hormones, frequency of use, and reversibility. The effectiveness tiers

The CDC organizes contraceptive methods into tiers by typical-use failure rate[3]Trussell, Contraceptive failure in the United States, Contraception 2011 · PMID 21477680. The framework is a useful starting point for shared decision-making, but the “best” method is the one you’ll actually use consistently and is medically safe for you.

Tier 1 — LARC + permanent (<1% failure)

  • Etonogestrel implant (Nexplanon) — 3-year implant in upper arm. Most effective reversible method.
  • Levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena 8 yr, Liletta 8 yr, Kyleena 5 yr, Skyla 3 yr).
  • Copper IUD (Paragard, 10 yr) — non-hormonal option.
  • Permanent: salpingectomy (laparoscopic, often combined with other GYN surgery).
  • Permanent: vasectomy (less invasive than salpingectomy).

Tier 2 — injection, pill, patch, ring (6–12% typical-use failure)

  • DMPA injection (Depo-Provera) — 13-week injection. Progestin-only.
  • Combined hormonal contraceptives — pill (daily), patch (weekly), ring (3-week / 1 year).
  • Progestin-only pill (norethindrone or drospirenone) — daily, narrow window.

Tier 3 — barriers + behavioral (18–30% typical-use failure)

  • Male condom — also protects against STIs.
  • Female condom, diaphragm, cervical cap.
  • Fertility awareness-based methods (calendar, basal body temperature, cervical mucus).
  • Withdrawal.
  • Spermicides alone (most effective combined with barrier).

Emergency contraception (after unprotected sex)

  • Copper IUD — most effective EC method; can also be left in place for ongoing contraception.
  • Ulipristal acetate (Ella) — oral, prescription, effective up to 120 hours.
  • Levonorgestrel (Plan B) — OTC, most effective within 72 hours.
  • All EC methods work by delaying ovulation, NOT by preventing implantation.

How to choose

Shared decision-making is the standard approach. Effectiveness matters, but so do many other things — side effects, privacy, cost, control over starting and stopping, fertility plans, and personal values about hormones.

Most common patient questions

What’s the most effective contraception?

LARC methods (implant and IUDs) — less than 1% failure with typical use[1]Winner et al., Effectiveness of long-acting reversible contraception, N Engl J Med 2012 · PMID 22621627. The implant slightly edges out IUDs in some efficacy data, but all Tier 1 methods are highly effective.

Do hormonal contraceptives cause weight gain?

The honest answer: DMPA injection has the most consistent weight-gain signal in studies. Combined hormonal contraceptives, implants, and IUDs do NOT cause weight gain on average in randomized data — individual women may notice fluctuations, but population-level effect is minimal. The progestin-only pill also has minimal effect.

Does the IUD hurt going in?

For most patients, insertion is uncomfortable but brief — intense cramping for 30–60 seconds, settling within minutes. Modern protocols include pre-medication (NSAIDs taken 30–60 minutes before), paracervical block when needed, and procedural sedation in selected cases. Discuss your concerns ahead of insertion — pain management has improved substantially.

Can I get pregnant right after stopping?

For most methods, yes — fertility returns within 1–3 cycles after pills, patch, ring, IUD, implant. The exception is DMPA injection — return to fertility averages 9–10 months after the last shot (range up to 18 months), so women planning pregnancy within a year should pick a different method.

What if I get pregnant with an IUD in place?

It’s rare (<1% chance), but when it happens, the priorities are: rule out ectopic pregnancy (relative risk of ectopic is higher with IUD in place, even though absolute risk is low), and remove the IUD if possible to reduce miscarriage and preterm birth risk if you’re continuing the pregnancy.

Can I use birth control to skip my period?

Yes — continuous use of combined hormonal contraceptives (skipping the placebo week) is safe and eliminates withdrawal bleeding entirely. Some women experience irregular spotting in the first 3–6 months that settles thereafter. Many patients in this practice use this approach for menstrual suppression with dysmenorrhea, endometriosis, or simply by preference.

What about emergency contraception?

Three main options[4]?, Practice Bulletin No. 152: Emergency Contraception, Obstet Gynecol 2015 · PMID 26287787: (1) copper IUD within 5 days — most effective and also provides ongoing contraception for 10 years; (2) ulipristal acetate (Ella) within 5 days, prescription; (3) levonorgestrel (Plan B) within 3 days, OTC. All work by delaying ovulation, not by interfering with an established pregnancy.

What conditions make some methods unsafe for me?

The US Medical Eligibility Criteria 2024[2]Nguyen et al., U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use, 2024, MMWR Recomm Rep 2024 · PMID 39106314 stratifies safety. Combined hormonal contraceptives (pills, patch, ring) are generally NOT recommended with: smoking over age 35, migraine with aura, uncontrolled hypertension, current or recent VTE, complex heart disease, certain liver disease, history of estrogen-sensitive breast cancer. Progestin-only options (mini-pill, DMPA, LARC) are usually safe in these settings, though some have their own restrictions. Your full medical history determines the right method.
