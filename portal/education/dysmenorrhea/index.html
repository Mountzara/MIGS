Painful periods — what’s normal, what isn’t, and what we can do.

Dysmenorrhea — pain with menstrual periods — is one of the most common reasons women see a gynecologist, and it is not something women should be told to live with. Primary vs secondary — the most important distinction

Primary dysmenorrhea is menstrual pain in the absence of identifiable pelvic pathology. It’s the physiologic kind — prostaglandin-driven uterine cramping. Classic features: starts within 1–2 years of menarche, present at every cycle, lasts the first 2–3 days of bleeding, lower abdominal cramping (sometimes with back ache or thigh pain), responds well to NSAIDs and to cycle suppression.

Secondary dysmenorrhea is menstrual pain caused by an underlying condition — endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, ovarian cysts, pelvic inflammatory disease, cervical stenosis, congenital uterine anomalies. Features that distinguish secondary from primary:

  • Pain that started later in life (not at menarche).
  • Worsening over time rather than stable.
  • Pain that lasts longer than the first 2–3 days — into mid-cycle or constantly.
  • Painful intercourse (dyspareunia), painful bowel movements (dyschezia), or painful urination (dysuria) accompanying the menstrual pain.
  • Pain that no longer responds to NSAIDs or hormonal suppression.
  • Heavy menstrual bleeding or irregular bleeding alongside the pain.
  • Infertility or recurrent pregnancy loss.

Endometriosis is the most common secondary cause[1]?, ACOG Committee Opinion No. 760: Dysmenorrhea and Endometriosis in t..., Obstet Gynecol 2018 · PMID 30461694, affecting about 10% of reproductive-age women. Adenomyosis is increasingly recognized as a co-existing or distinct cause. Fibroids contribute when they distort the cavity or are large enough to cause bulk symptoms.

How dysmenorrhea gets evaluated

The history and exam usually determine whether the pain is likely primary or secondary, and what additional workup is needed. The framework:

  1. History. Age at onset of dysmenorrhea (years post-menarche vs later); pattern (cyclic only vs constant); severity (work/school missed, sleep disruption); associated symptoms (dyspareunia, dyschezia, dysuria, GI changes with periods); response to NSAIDs and hormonal options; family history of endometriosis; reproductive history; bleeding pattern.
  2. Pelvic exam. Speculum to visualize cervix and vagina; bimanual exam for uterine size and tenderness, adnexal masses, focal pelvic-floor tenderness, nodularity in the cul-de-sac (suggesting deep infiltrating endometriosis).
  3. Transvaginal ultrasound. First-line imaging — identifies fibroids, ovarian cysts (including endometriomas), adenomyosis features, congenital uterine anomalies.
  4. Pelvic MRI. When TVUS suggests deep infiltrating endometriosis, adenomyosis needs better characterization, or surgical planning is needed.
  5. Trial of treatment. For most patients with classic primary-dysmenorrhea history and a normal exam, the standard is a trial of NSAIDs and/or hormonal suppression for 3 cycles before considering further workup.
  6. Laparoscopy. Reserved for patients whose pain persists despite a real medical-therapy trial, or when imaging suggests a surgical lesion needs to be addressed[7]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458. It’s no longer needed to make the diagnosis of endometriosis — clinical features + imaging are sufficient.

The treatment ladder

Most women get meaningful relief from first-line medical options. Escalation happens only when the basics don’t work.

1

NSAIDs — the foundation

Pre-emptively dosed (start the day before expected menses, continue through the first 2–3 days). Ibuprofen 600–800 mg every 8 hours, or naproxen 500 mg twice daily, with food[2]Marjoribanks et al., Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for dysmenorrhoea, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2015 · PMID 26224322. Up to 75% of women get adequate relief.

2

Hormonal suppression

Combined hormonal contraceptives reduce pain significantly[3]Wong et al., Oral contraceptive pill for primary dysmenorrhoea, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2009 · PMID 19821293. Continuous use (skipping the placebo week) eliminates withdrawal bleeding entirely. Progestin-only options (oral, LNG-IUD, depot) are alternatives when estrogen is contraindicated.

3

Pelvic-floor PT

For pain with a pelvic-floor or musculoskeletal component, specialized pelvic-floor physical therapy is highly effective. Particularly when intercourse is painful or pelvic-floor tightness is part of the picture.

4

OMT & complementary

Osteopathic manipulative treatment has RCT support for endometriosis-associated CPP[5]Molins-Cubero et al., Changes in pain perception after pelvis manipulation in women with ..., Pain Med 2014 · PMID 24666560. Acupuncture, TENS, heat therapy, and exercise have supporting evidence for symptom reduction[6]Smith et al., Acupuncture for dysmenorrhoea, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2016 · PMID 27087494.

5

GnRH antagonists

Oral elagolix 150 mg daily (with add-back) is FDA-approved for endometriosis-associated dysmenorrhea[4]Taylor et al., Treatment of Endometriosis-Associated Pain with Elagolix, an Oral G..., N Engl J Med 2017 · PMID 28525302. Reserved for refractory pain after first-line options.

!

When to suspect endometriosis

Dysmenorrhea unresponsive to 3 months of NSAIDs + hormonal therapy, dyspareunia, dyschezia, infertility, or imaging findings of endometrioma all warrant evaluation for endometriosis as the underlying cause.

Most common patient questions

Is bad period pain normal?

Some discomfort is — periods involve uterine contractions, and prostaglandin release causes the cramping. But pain severe enough to interfere with work, school, sleep, or daily activities is not something women should be told to live with. About half of menstruating women experience dysmenorrhea, and 10–15% have it severely enough to disrupt life — that’s the threshold for evaluation and treatment.

How do I know if my pain is from endometriosis?

Endometriosis is the most likely secondary cause of dysmenorrhea, affecting ~10% of reproductive-age women[1]?, ACOG Committee Opinion No. 760: Dysmenorrhea and Endometriosis in t..., Obstet Gynecol 2018 · PMID 30461694. Suspicious features: pain that started later in life (not at menarche), worsens over time, lasts longer than the first 2–3 days, is accompanied by painful intercourse, painful bowel movements, or infertility, and doesn’t respond to NSAIDs and hormonal options. The diagnosis is increasingly made clinically (without surgery) when these features are present and imaging is consistent. See the endometriosis guide for the full evaluation.

Will birth control fix my pain?

Often, yes. Combined hormonal contraceptives reduce dysmenorrhea pain significantly[3]Wong et al., Oral contraceptive pill for primary dysmenorrhoea, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2009 · PMID 19821293, and using them continuously (skipping the placebo week) eliminates withdrawal bleeding entirely. Many women find that continuous OCP, the levonorgestrel IUD, or the depo injection resolves their pain. The right choice depends on your health profile, fertility plans, and preferences.

What about NSAIDs — how should I take them?

Pre-emptively, not reactively. Start the day before you expect your period and continue every 8 hours with food through the first 2–3 days. Ibuprofen 600–800 mg three times daily or naproxen 500 mg twice daily are the typical regimens[2]Marjoribanks et al., Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for dysmenorrhoea, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2015 · PMID 26224322. The window matters — once severe pain has developed, NSAIDs are less effective. Caution if you have GI ulcer disease, kidney disease, asthma exacerbations from NSAIDs, or are on anticoagulation.

What about “just stopping periods”?

Continuous menstrual suppression is safe — menstruation (ovulation followed by withdrawal bleeding) is not physiologically necessary for women not trying to conceive. Continuous combined OCP, the LNG-IUD, depo-medroxyprogesterone, and other options can eliminate periods entirely for women who want that. The breakthrough bleeding that sometimes happens early on usually settles within 3–6 months.

Does pelvic-floor PT actually help?

For dysmenorrhea with a musculoskeletal component (high-tone pelvic floor, low back ache, hip pain that worsens with periods), specialized pelvic-floor physical therapy is very effective. It particularly helps when intercourse is painful. A general orthopedic PT is not the same — insist on a pelvic-floor specialist.

Will I need surgery?

Almost never for primary dysmenorrhea. Surgery is reserved for cases where (a) imaging shows a structural lesion that needs removal (endometrioma, large fibroid, large polyp), (b) pain persists despite a real trial of medical therapy and other secondary-cause workup, or (c) the patient wants tissue diagnosis. Cochrane data support laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis-associated pain[7]Bafort et al., Laparoscopic surgery for endometriosis, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2020 · PMID 33095458, but it is rarely the first answer.

Criteria for triage tests were set at sensitivity ≥ 95% and specificity ≥ 50%, ruling out the diagnosis with a negative result (SnNout test - if sensitivity is high, a negative test rules out pathology) or at sensitivity ≥ 50% with specificity ≥ 95%, ruling in the diagnosis with a positive result (SpPin test - if specificity is high, a positive test rules in pathology).

    MAIN RESULTS: We included 49 studies involving 4807 women: 13 studies evaluated pelvic endometriosis, 10 endometriomas and 15 DIE, and 33 studies addressed endometriosis at specific anatomical sites. Most studies were of poor methodological quality. The most studied modalities were transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with outcome measures commonly demonstrating diversity in diagnostic estimates; however, sources of heterogeneity could not be reliably determined. No imaging test met the criteria for a replacement or triage test for detecting pelvic endometriosis, albeit TVUS approached the criteria for a SpPin triage test. For endometrioma, TVUS (eight studies, 765 participants; sensitivity 0.93 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.87, 0.99), specificity 0.96 (95% CI 0.92, 0.99)) qualified as a SpPin triage test and approached the criteria for a replacement and SnNout triage test, whereas MRI (three studies, 179 participants; sensitivity 0.95 (95% CI 0.90, 1.00), specificity 0.91 (95% CI 0.86, 0.97)) met the criteria for a replacement and SnNout triage test and approached the criteria for a SpPin test. For DIE, TVUS (nine studies, 12 data sets, 934 participants; sensitivity 0.79 (95% CI 0.69, 0.89) and specificity 0.94 (95% CI 0.88, 1.00)) approached the criteria for a SpPin triage test, and MRI (six studies, seven data sets, 266 participants; sensitivity 0.94 (95% CI 0.90, 0.97), specificity 0.77 (95% CI 0.44, 1.00)) approached the criteria for a replacement and SnNout triage test. Other imaging tests assessed in small individual studies could not be statistically evaluated.TVUS met the criteria for a SpPin triage test in mapping DIE to uterosacral ligaments, rectovaginal septum, vaginal wall, pouch of Douglas (POD) and rectosigmoid. MRI met the criteria for a SpPin triage test for POD and vaginal and rectosigmoid endometriosis. Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) might qualify as a SpPin triage test for rectosigmoid involvement but could not be adequately assessed for other anatomical sites because heterogeneous data were scant. Multi-detector computerised tomography enema (MDCT-e) displayed the highest diagnostic performance for rectosigmoid and other bowel endometriosis and met the criteria for both SpPin and SnNout triage tests, but studies were too few to provide meaningful results.Diagnostic accuracies were higher for TVUS with bowel preparation (TVUS-BP) and rectal water contrast (RWC-TVS) and for 3.0TMRI than for conventional methods, although the paucity of studies precluded statistical evaluation.

    AUTHORS' CONCLUSIONS: None of the evaluated imaging modalities were able to detect overall pelvic endometriosis with enough accuracy that they would be suggested to replace surgery. Specifically for endometrioma, TVUS qualified as a SpPin triage test. MRI displayed sufficient accuracy to suggest utility as a replacement test, but the data were too scant to permit meaningful conclusions. TVUS could be used clinically to identify additional anatomical sites of DIE compared with MRI, thus facilitating preoperative planning. Rectosigmoid endometriosis was the only site that could be accurately mapped by using TVUS, TRUS, MRI or MDCT-e. Studies evaluating recent advances in imaging modalities such as TVUS-BP, RWC-TVS, 3.0TMRI and MDCT-e were observed to have high diagnostic accuracies but were too few to allow prudent evaluation of their diagnostic role. In view of the low quality of most of the included studies, the findings of this review should be interpreted with caution. Future well-designed diagnostic studies undertaken to compare imaging tests for diagnostic test accuracy and costs are recommended.
