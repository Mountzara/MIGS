Module 1 of 6 · Understanding Endometriosis: What It Is and Why It Hurts

The three faces of the disease — and why size doesn't equal pain

Maybe someone once glanced at your scan and pronounced your case 'mild,' while your life kept shrinking around the pain. The word didn't match what you were living.

Superficial peritoneal implants

The most common form of endometriosis — small patches of endometriotic tissue scattered across the peritoneum (the thin tissue that lines the pelvic cavity). Lesions can be red, dark, white, or non-pigmented depending on age and activity.

How it shows up: dysmenorrhea, deep dyspareunia, and chronic pelvic pain with often-normal-looking imaging. Ovarian endometriomas (“chocolate cysts”)

Cysts on the ovary lined by endometriotic tissue and filled with old, dark blood — sometimes called “chocolate cysts” for their characteristic appearance. Highly visible on transvaginal ultrasound with the classic ground-glass echogenicity (sensitivity 83–93%, specificity 89–97%)[5]Transvaginal ultrasound vs magnetic resonance imaging for diagnosing deep infiltrating endometriosis: systematic review and meta-analysis.Ultrasound Obstet Gynecol 2018 · PMID 29154402Guerriero et al., Ultrasound Obstet Gynecol — TVUS and MRI have comparable accuracy for deep infiltrating endometriosis when performed by trained operators..

Why they matter: endometriomas reduce ovarian reserve over time (which is why we check an AMH early), can rupture, and can become very large. The largest concern is their association with reduced fertility potential and with pelvic adhesion formation.

Deep infiltrating endometriosis (DIE)

Lesions that grow more than 5 mm beneath the peritoneal surface — into the bowel wall, the bladder wall, the rectovaginal septum, or the ligaments behind the uterus (the uterosacral ligaments). Causes the most severe symptoms and usually requires detailed MRI mapping before any surgery is planned.

How it shows up: pain with bowel movements (dyschezia), pain with urination, deep dyspareunia in specific positions, cyclic rectal bleeding, cyclic hematuria, or a fixed retroverted uterus on exam.

One thing that surprises most people: the severity of symptoms does not match the visible amount of disease. It feels like common sense — with most illnesses, more disease means more symptoms, so mild pain should mean mild disease. Endometriosis breaks that rule: the severity of symptoms does not match the visible amount of disease.
