Module 1 of 6 · Understanding Endometriosis: What It Is and Why It Hurts

Four overlapping pain sources, all real, all physical

You've tried explaining that the pain isn't one thing — it moves, it changes character, it shows up on days it has no business showing up. Mostly, you've watched that explanation get flattened into a single word: cramps.

Why it hurts

One of the reasons endometriosis is so often under-treated is that pain in this condition is never coming from just one place. Treating only the lesions — with hormones or even with surgery — leaves the other three layers untreated, which is why so many women continue to hurt after “the surgery went well.”

From the clinic counseling script, verbatim

In endometriosis, pain comes from at least four overlapping sources simultaneously: the lesions themselves releasing inflammatory chemicals like prostaglandins and cytokines; the nerves around the lesions, which become abnormally sensitive over time; the pelvic-floor muscles, which develop trigger points and tightness as a guarding response; and the fascia and ligaments — especially the broad ligament and uterosacral ligaments — which can become restricted by adhesions and inflammation. Hormonal therapy reduces the activity of the lesions but does not directly address the muscle tightness, the fascial restriction, or the sensitized pain pathways — which is why some women continue to have pain even after good hormonal control or after excision surgery.

Layer one — the lesions themselves

Each endometriotic implant releases prostaglandins (especially PGE2 and PGF2α) and pro-inflammatory cytokines — the same chemical signals that drive ordinary menstrual cramps, only produced constantly and locally rather than cyclically. This is what produces the classic cyclic pain pattern early in the disease.

What targets this layer: NSAIDs (which inhibit prostaglandin synthesis directly) and continuous hormonal contraception (which suppresses ovulation and the cyclic hormonal trigger that drives lesion activity).
